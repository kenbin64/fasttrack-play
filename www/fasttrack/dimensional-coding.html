The Geometry of Fast Track

z = x · y

The whole game is built from one surface. This page states what the math actually confirms, what the geometry bought us, and runs the benchmarks live in your browser — so you do not have to take any of it on faith.

0One primitive, not a pile of features

Fast Track is a 3D peg-racing board game. Under the felt it is a single mathematical object iterated: the saddle z = x·y, a hyperbolic paraboloid.

Three facts make this surface special, and each one earns its keep later:

It is harmonic. Its Laplacian is zero — the simplest non-trivial saddle solution of Laplace's equation. It is literally the imaginary part of a complex square, x·y = Im((x + iy)²) / 2, so the primitive already lives inside complex structure.

It is doubly ruled. Fix x and z is a straight line in y; fix y and it is straight in x. A curved saddle built entirely from straight lines. This is the property that later makes a move's outcome free to read off.

It tiles into the board. Over the square [-1,1]² the saddle is inscribed exactly in a cube, its four corners land on alternating cube vertices — a regular tetrahedron — and its four straight edges frame the Schwarz Diamond minimal surface. The hexagonal board, its safe zones and its bullseye are assemblies of this one patch.

1Representation, not compression

The core idea. We do not store data — we store the rule that extracts it. We elect one congressman for thousands; we do not compress the thousands into him.

Compression is reconstructive: it replays what you stored, bounded by its input. Representation is generative: it answers questions never in the data — finer resolution, points between the samples, points outside the original domain. The samples were always derivative; the rule was always primary.

Benchmark — a bilinear image is z = x·y

A colour gradient defined by four corner colours, interpolated bilinearly, is exactly the saddle in colour space. We reconstruct every pixel from just the four corners and measure the error against the full image. Computed in your browser, just now:

Benchmark — 88 piano keys from 2 numbers

A musical scale is a rule, not a table: f(n) = 440 · 2^((n − 48)/12). Two numbers regenerate the whole keyboard. (Fast Track's procedural music is built this way.)

Fence. Most data has no short generator — that is the incompressibility limit (Kolmogorov / Shannon), and it is real. The image above hit zero error only because it genuinely was bilinear. Real signals lie only approximately on a low-dimensional manifold, so on real data the method trades fidelity for size. We claim the structured case, not magic.

2The board is derived, not drawn

Fast Track's board is not a hand-placed list of ~150 holes. It is generated from a handful of golden-ratio constants and the six-fold symmetry of the hexagon. The rules live in fasttrack.rules.json as a single source of truth; the geometry falls out of a generator.

Benchmark — board holes from a few constants

Six wedges × 14 outer holes, six fast-track ring holes, four safe-zone holes and five holding/home holes per player, one bullseye. The renderer never stores hole positions; it asks the generator for them — which means the same board scales cleanly from 2 to 6 players.

3The quality manifold: graphics that give

Fast Track ran beautifully on a strong desktop and clunked on weak laptops and phones. The old switch was binary — full billiard room or stripped void. The fix is the representation idea pointed at performance.

We do not store quality presets as a lookup table. We store the generator. One measured capability scalar — c ∈ [0,1], your machine's power — regenerates the entire render-settings vector. The presets were always derivative; the rule settingsFor(c) is primary.

effective quality = capability(machine) · demand(scene) // z = x·y again

Capability is combined as a product (a geometric mean of GPU, CPU, and memory), not an average — because z = x·y says one weak factor must collapse the whole. A software GPU caps quality no matter how many CPU cores you have. That is the correct physics.

4The governor: a recursion that survives

Detection gets you a starting point. But thermal throttling, a busy tab, or a heavier scene can still drop frames. So the quality scalar is also a feedback loop — and feedback loops are where naive recursion explodes.

Raw multiplicative recursion (multiply the state by a factor each step) is unstable: it runs away or collapses to zero. The stable form is additive — write a small correction onto a mostly-unchanged state and renormalise. That is the residual connection, the same trick that lets a 60-layer network stay bounded. Our governor uses exactly that shape:

c ← clamp( c + α · (target_frame_time − measured_frame_time), floor, ceiling )

Small steps toward a frame-time target, clamped between a floor (below which degrading further would stop being the game) and the machine's detected ceiling (it never invents quality the hardware cannot deliver). It cuts faster than it recovers, so it settles instead of oscillating.

Frame-time meter (this page, right now)

The same meter runs inside the game. When it sees sustained slow frames it eases the pixel ratio and shadows down a notch; when it sees headroom it eases them back toward your machine's ceiling.

6The fences (stated in our own voice)

Incompressibility is real. Structured data has short generators; random data does not. We optimise the structured parts and say so.
The cost relocates, it does not vanish. Making the move outcome a free multiply just moves all the work into getting the retrieved factor right. The geometry that is cheap to read is not cheap to learn.
Detection is a heuristic. A masked GPU string or an unusual device can fool the probe — which is exactly why the runtime governor exists: it corrects the guess against real measured frames.
Adaptation is bounded. The governor only moves the cheap knobs live (pixel ratio, shadows). Heavier settings apply on the next load; we never tear down the scene mid-game.
